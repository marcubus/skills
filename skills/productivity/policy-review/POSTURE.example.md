# Posture

The standard a policy is measured against.

## Context

Who is covered: you and your household.

What is being protected: personal, household, location, purchase and browsing, health, employment and applicant data.

Jurisdiction: Australia, unless you change it.

## Hard gates

### G1. No sale or third-party monetisation

Personal information must not be sold, rented, or shared for a recipient's own marketing. Silence fails. "Business partners for their own purposes" fails even when softened by "trusted".

### G2. AI training opt-in only

Customer content and behaviour must not train models without explicit opt-in. Opt-out buried in account settings or consent implied by use fails. "We may use your information to improve our services" reads as training permitted.

### G3. Cross-border accountability

Overseas recipients must be named or nameable, with the transfer mechanism stated. An open-ended country list that may change without notice fails. A closed list with contractual accountability passes. Silence fails.

### G4. Retention maxima

Stated maximum retention periods, or a stated rule that binds each category, plus a destruction or de-identification commitment. "As long as necessary" alone fails. A deletion request must be honoured within 30 days. A missing limb fails the gate.

### G5. Breach notification

Commitment to the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme and to notifying affected individuals, not just the regulator. A policy that names security measures and disclaims guarantees without mentioning notification fails.

### G6. Children and minors

Collection limited to operational necessity. Advertising use of minors' data excluded. Household-level collection that captures children's data must be addressed. Silence on minors in a policy that collects household composition fails.

## Soft preferences

Weighted by exposure. They rank the flags.

### S1. Collection minimisation

Categories with no stated purpose, inferred attributes, household or device fingerprint collection.

### S2. Sensitive information consent

Health, biometric, racial, political, sexual orientation, criminal record. Explicit consent and a stated purpose required. Consent is described when the policy names an opt-in. It is assumed when the policy relies on use of the service.

### S3. Purpose limitation

Width of "related purposes" and "other purposes we tell you about". Broad related-purpose language is the mechanism by which the stated purpose stops meaning anything.

### S4. Recipient transparency

Named classes beat unnamed ones. "Service providers" alone is weak. "Advertising networks and data partners" is a disclosure worth flagging.

### S5. Security specificity

Named measures are checkable. Generic assurances are not. Quote a disclaimer that undercuts the named measures, because it usually contradicts them.

### S6. Automated decisions

Disclosure of the kinds of information used, the decisions made solely by a program, and the decisions a program assists. Check for a human review path. "Contact us" is not a mechanism. Under APP 1.7 to 1.9 the obligation applies from the statutory commencement date.

### S7. Access and correction mechanics

A stated timeframe and a dispute mechanism. Correction is free. The note-of-dispute right (APP 13.4) should be present.

### S8. Marketing consent and withdrawal symmetry

Withdrawal as easy as consent. Transactional carve-outs honest. Consent not a precondition of service.

### S9. Change notification

Commit to telling people before a material change takes effect, not only posting the new version. Posting only is the common and weaker form.

### S10. Document integrity

Broken cross-references, contradictory sections, web versus PDF divergence. Quote the defect rather than describing it.

### S11. Accountability surface

Named privacy officer, real contact address, complaint response commitment. An anonymous web form is a flag.
